Look-Ahead Resource Allocation for Emergency Departments Under Shifting Demand: A Multi-Agent Decision-Support Prototype

Aryan Jadhav · Atharva Falle · Pratish  |  Decision-support prototype · synthetic data only · plan stage, no results yet

Open the PDF3 pages · same content as this page

1Problem & Theme Chosen

We chose “Splitting a Shared Resource When Demand Keeps Changing,” studied in a hospital emergency department (ED). Patients of different urgency compete for limited general, oxygen-supported and ICU beds, ventilators and staff while arrivals surge unpredictably. The system is a decision-support prototype using synthetic data; clinicians retain every real decision and we claim no clinical validity.

2Understanding of the Challenge

A fixed rule is tuned for yesterday’s demand: giving the last ICU bed to the first eligible patient leaves none when sicker patients arrive minutes later. Allocation must weigh future demand, not only the queue. Hinson et al. (JAMA Intern Med 2024; 12,147 chest-pain visits, 3 EDs) and a scoping review of AI in ED triage (PubMed 39127019) treat AI urgency scoring as decision support and warn of overconfident predictions and poor transfer between hospitals. A waiting-time study on two Italian hospitals (40,504 and 60,230 cases) reports that queue-state features and ensemble models cut forecast error by 23–28%. Because an empty bed may lack oxygen, monitoring or staff, we allocate resource bundles, not bed counts.

3Proposed Approach

A. System modules (Figure 1)

ModuleFunction
Patient Data CollectionGathers patient details, symptoms, vital signs and history.
AI Triage EngineAnalyses the information and suggests an urgency category; starts as a transparent rule-based score, upgradeable to a small ML classifier.
Prioritization EngineOrders the queue by urgency.
Resource Allocation EngineFinds suitable beds, ICU capacity, staff and equipment.
DatabaseStores patient records, triage output and resource availability.
DashboardShows patient queues, available resources and shortage alerts.
Staff ApprovalLets clinicians review, confirm or override every decision; overrides are logged.

The design is two cooperating agents (triage, allocation). If independent actors are required, ED, ward and ICU units become separate agents requesting from the shared pool.

B. Allocation methodology

Baseline: a rule-based allocator assigns patients by urgency, then arrival time, to the first compatible free bed. Proposed allocator: receding-horizon planning (MPC). At each step it:

  1. estimates the arrival rate from a moving window and flags shocks with a change-detection test such as CUSUM;
  2. simulates the next H hours for several candidate policies (for example, different ICU/oxygen reserve levels);
  3. scores each by urgency-weighted waiting time plus penalties for unserved critical patients;
  4. applies only the first decision, then re-plans.

Hard constraints Critical patients are never displaced by lower-urgency ones, and stale capacity data raises an alert. We prefer planning to reinforcement learning because a beginner team can debug it; PPO is the alternative.

C. Simulation environment

A custom Gymnasium-compatible simulator generates synthetic patients with time-varying Poisson arrivals, starting at 10 general, 4 oxygen and 2 ICU beds, 1 ventilator, 3 doctors and 8 nurses.

D. Supplementary Bao-inspired module (separate from the core system)

Bao (Marcus et al., SIGMOD 2021) is a bandit wrapper using a tree-convolutional network and Thompson sampling to choose query hint sets, with fallback to the native optimizer. Our module is only Bao-inspired: a query optimizer that compares query variants and selects by measured execution time. It does not aim to reproduce the network or Thompson sampling, is separate from triage and carries none of our core claims. Intended stack: HTML, CSS and JavaScript frontend; Node.js and Express.js backend; MongoDB Atlas database; hospital management dashboard. The existing code has not yet been audited.

Target Lower urgency-weighted and 95th-percentile waiting time without worse critical-patient outcomes, claimed only if it holds across at least 30 seeds with confidence intervals.

Fallback A one-step lookahead reserving an ICU/oxygen margin from forecast demand.

5Stretch Ambitions

  1. One run with both an abrupt shock and gradual drift, testing reaction to a jump without over-reacting to noise.
  2. Long-horizon evaluation (simulated weeks) to expose behaviour that short episodes hide.

6Novelty/Differentiation

We claim no new algorithm; receding-horizon planning and ED queue forecasting are established. Our contribution is an application-specific integration: bundle-aware allocation with explicit look-ahead, tested under shock and drift in one long run against a fair baseline. The cited sources study triage and waiting-time prediction separately; the combined workflow is our proposed extension.
